Demandbase AEO Agent: What It Does, and What You Can Track for Free

Demandbase just launched an AI agent that tracks how ChatGPT, Gemini and other assistants describe your brand. We dug into what it actually does, and set it next to the free method we've been running ourselves for two months.

BR
The BuzzRiding TeamResearched and refined with AI tools

On 20 October, Demandbase stood on stage at its GO conference in New York and launched three AI agents. One of them, the AEO Agent, does something this site has been doing by hand every month since September: it checks what AI assistants say about a brand, and who they cite when they say it.

The difference is that Demandbase is selling it as an enterprise product, built on a dataset of 342 million contacts across 13 million companies. We have been doing a rougher version of the same thing for free, with the raw data published alongside every run. This is what the announcement actually says, what it leaves out, and where a paid agent earns its price over a DIY habit.

What Demandbase announced

The AEO Agent is one of three agents Demandbase launched alongside a Campaign Agent and an Audience Agent, all built on its existing Mojo platform. According to MarTech Edge's report on the launch, the AEO Agent lets marketers compare how major AI assistants describe their brand, check visibility for relevant buyer questions, and identify the sources shaping those AI-generated answers. It also flags outdated information and content gaps, then helps launch campaigns to close them.

Demandbase CEO Gabe Rogol framed the stakes bluntly in comments reported by destinationCRM: "They start with an agent. And those agents are deciding which brands get seen and which get overlooked." The same report says the agent can identify anonymous AI-driven traffic at the account and contact level, so sales can follow up on buyers an AI assistant already pointed toward a competitor.

That account-matching piece is the part a solo marketer cannot replicate. Knowing that ChatGPT mentioned your category is useful. Knowing which named account asked the question, pulled from a 13-million-company graph, is a different product entirely.

The other two agents launched alongside it are worth a sentence each, because they show what Demandbase is actually selling: a connected system, not three separate tools. The Campaign Agent ties campaign activity back to revenue targets and flags underperforming spend. The Audience Agent builds and maintains buyer lists from ideal-customer profiles and flags missing members of a buying group. The AEO Agent feeds both of them: a brand's AI-answer visibility becomes an input the other two agents can act on, launching campaigns or updating audiences without a marketer stitching the data together by hand.

What the announcement leaves out

Neither report names which AI assistants the agent actually monitors. Neither mentions pricing, a free tier, or a self-serve signup. Access runs through Demandbase's existing platform and its Mojo architecture, reachable via APIs and Model Context Protocol connections, which points at an enterprise sales conversation rather than a product page with a "Start free trial" button.

There is also a pattern worth flagging. Covering an earlier round of Demandbase agent launches, Diginomica reported that Demandbase's own CEO described the agent category as heading toward "the trough of disillusionment," and that performance figures the company cited came with no published methodology. The same piece noted an earlier design-partner program was capped at 30 companies. None of that is about the AEO Agent specifically, since it predates this launch, but it is the vendor's own track record on how it talks about what its agents deliver.

Put plainly: the AEO Agent's description matches a real, useful job. The evidence for how well it does that job, so far, is one CEO quote and a conference stage.

The free version of the same idea

We cannot test Demandbase's agent. It is not self-serve, and we are not an enterprise account with a buying committee. What we can show is what the first half of its job, tracking what AI assistants cite, looks like done by hand, for free, with the receipts published.

In September we ran eight fixed prompts through Google's AI Mode, logged out, and recorded every cited domain: 55 citations across 48 sites, and zero mentions of BuzzRiding. In October we ran the same eight prompts again to see what moved. Both runs, including the raw citation lists, are committed to this site's public repository, not summarized into a dashboard only we can see.

🔬 What this cost

Two monthly runs, roughly an hour each, using a logged-out browser and a spreadsheet. No contract, no sales call, no named pricing tier. The full method is in our free GEO-tracking workflow.

That is genuinely a smaller job than what Demandbase is selling. We are not matching anonymous AI traffic to named accounts, we are not flagging content gaps automatically, and we are not launching campaigns from inside the same tool. We are answering one question: which sites does an AI assistant cite on topics that matter to us, and does that change month to month.

It does change. Between the two runs, the set of domains AI Mode cited on our eight prompts shifted noticeably, and the only two sources cited in both runs were YouTube and LinkedIn, the same platforms that keep showing up across unrelated queries in our data. That single comparison, free, took about an hour of browser time and a spreadsheet. It is a smaller claim than "AEO Agent," and it is one we can fully show our work on.

Where the paid version earns its price

We already reviewed three paid tools built for exactly this gap, HubSpot's AEO grader, Otterly.ai, Profound and Mangools, and found real differences in how much of this they automate versus what a free workflow still requires by hand. Demandbase's pitch sits a level above that tier again: it is not just automating the citation check, it is tying brand mentions to a named-account sales motion, something none of those four tools claim to do.

Lay the four approaches next to each other and a pattern shows up:

  • Free, logged-out AI Mode runs — citation visibility only, manual, monthly, zero cost, fully reproducible by a reader.
  • HubSpot / Otterly.ai / Profound / Mangools — automated citation and share-of-voice tracking across more assistants, a real subscription cost, no account-matching.
  • Demandbase AEO Agent — citation visibility plus anonymous-traffic-to-named-account matching plus campaign activation, enterprise sales process, no published price.

For a solo marketer or a small team, that account-matching layer is overkill. The free method answers "are we visible, and to whom" well enough to act on. For a B2B marketing org with a sales team that wants to know which specific account an AI assistant just steered toward a competitor, that is a different budget line, and arguably a fair one, assuming the account-matching actually works as described.

None of the three report on this launch, including Demandbase's own site, publishes an independent case study with numbers attached. Until one does, "assuming it works as described" is doing real work in that sentence.

One naming collision worth flagging

Searching for "AEO Agent" currently surfaces an unrelated product called simply AEO, listed on Product Hunt around the same week. It is not affiliated with Demandbase. If you are researching this, check the vendor name in the result before you read further, the same ambiguity check we run before briefing any article here.

The bottom line

Demandbase's AEO Agent describes a real and growing problem correctly: brands have no visibility into what AI assistants say about them, and that is now a buying-journey stage nobody tracks by default. What it does not yet have, publicly, is pricing, named platform coverage, or independent results. Until one of those shows up, the free version, run monthly and published honestly, is the only one of the two anyone outside Demandbase's sales team can actually verify.

That is not an argument against the paid tier existing. A 13-million-company contact graph and a sales-activation layer are not things a spreadsheet replicates, and for a team that already lives inside Demandbase's platform, bolting on answer-engine tracking is a reasonable next purchase. The argument is narrower: do not let the size of the launch convince you that visibility tracking itself requires an enterprise budget. It does not, yet, and the gap between the two is exactly the part nobody in this launch's coverage has put a number on.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is Demandbase's AEO Agent?
An AI agent Demandbase launched on 20 October 2026 that tracks how AI assistants describe and recommend a brand, identifies the sources behind those answers, and matches anonymous AI-driven traffic to named accounts for sales follow-up.
How much does Demandbase's AEO Agent cost?
Neither Demandbase nor the press coverage of the launch has published pricing. Access runs through Demandbase's existing platform, which is sold as an enterprise product rather than a self-serve signup.
Can I track AI answer-engine visibility without Demandbase?
Yes. Running a fixed set of prompts through an AI assistant's logged-out search mode and recording the cited domains, repeated monthly, gets you most of the visibility picture for free. It will not match that visibility to named accounts the way an enterprise tool can.
Which AI assistants does the AEO Agent monitor?
Public coverage of the launch describes "major AI assistants" without naming which ones. Demandbase has not published a list.
Is the AEO Agent the same as the AEO app on Product Hunt?
No. They are unrelated products that happen to share a name and launched in the same week. Check the vendor before citing either one.
